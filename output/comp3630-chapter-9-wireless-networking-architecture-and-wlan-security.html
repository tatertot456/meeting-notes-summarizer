RF Propagation & Antenna Fundamentals

▶

Wi-Fi transmits data using radio frequency (RF) electromagnetic waves. Understanding how RF energy moves through space and physical obstacles is essential for designing reliable WLANs.

Frequency

Higher frequencies (5 GHz, 6 GHz) carry more data but attenuate faster. Lower frequencies (2.4 GHz) travel farther but have less bandwidth capacity.

Attenuation

Signal strength decreases with distance and obstruction. Concrete and metal cause heavy attenuation; drywall and glass cause moderate loss.

Omnidirectional Antennas

Radiate RF energy equally in all horizontal directions. Used in most enterprise access points for general-area coverage.

Directional Antennas

Focus RF energy in a narrow beam for point-to-point links (building-to-building) or focused corridor coverage. Higher gain = narrower beam.

  • dBi (Gain): Antenna gain measured in decibels relative to an isotropic radiator. Higher dBi concentrates signal in a specific direction.
  • EIRP (Effective Isotropic Radiated Power): Transmit power + antenna gain. Regulated by the FCC to limit interference.
  • Multipath: RF signals reflect off surfaces and arrive at the receiver via multiple paths at different phases. Can cause signal cancellation or reinforcement.

Physical Layer: OFDMA, Channel Bonding & Modulation

▶

OFDM (Wi-Fi 4/5)

Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing splits a channel into parallel subcarriers. One client uses the entire channel per transmission slot.

OFDMA (Wi-Fi 6+)

Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access divides subcarriers into Resource Units (RUs) so multiple clients share one channel simultaneously. Dramatically reduces latency in high-density environments.

Channel Bonding

Combines adjacent 20 MHz channels into 40/80/160/320 MHz wide channels. Doubles bandwidth per bond; requires clean spectrum without interference.

QAM Modulation

Higher-order QAM encodes more bits per symbol. 256-QAM (Wi-Fi 5) = 8 bits/symbol. 1024-QAM (Wi-Fi 6) = 10 bits/symbol. 4096-QAM (Wi-Fi 7) = 12 bits/symbol. Requires strong signal (high SNR).

Target Wake Time (TWT): Wi-Fi 6 feature that schedules when IoT devices wake up to transmit, dramatically reducing power consumption and airtime contention in dense sensor deployments.

MIMO, MU-MIMO & Beamforming

▶

MIMO (Multiple Input / Multiple Output)

Uses multiple antennas to send and receive several spatial data streams simultaneously over the same channel, multiplying throughput without extra spectrum.

MU-MIMO (Multi-User MIMO)

Wi-Fi 5 added downlink MU-MIMO (AP transmits to multiple clients at once). Wi-Fi 6 added uplink MU-MIMO (multiple clients transmit to AP simultaneously).

Beamforming

AP adjusts phase and amplitude across its antenna array to concentrate RF energy toward a specific client, improving signal strength and reducing interference for that device.

Spatial Streams

Notation: 4x4:4 = 4 transmit antennas, 4 receive antennas, 4 spatial streams. More streams = higher peak throughput but requires the client to support the same count.

WLAN Site Survey Methodology & RSSI Design

▶

A site survey ensures APs are placed for correct coverage, capacity, and roaming before physical installation.

  • Passive Survey: Walk the floor with a laptop running Wi-Fi scanning software. Records RSSI, channel, and SNR of existing signals without transmitting.
  • Active Survey: Temporarily place APs at candidate locations and test real throughput and roaming behavior.
  • Predictive Survey: Import floor plan into RF modeling software to simulate AP placement before physical deployment.

Cell Overlap: Adjacent APs should have 15–20% coverage overlap on non-overlapping channels to ensure seamless roaming without gaps or dead zones.

Wireless Client Mobility & 802.11k/v/r Fast Roaming

▶

802.11k — Neighbor Reports

AP tells the client which nearby APs exist and their signal quality. Client builds a candidate roam list without having to scan all channels, speeding up handoff decisions.

802.11v — BSS Transition Management

AP can suggest or push a client to roam to a better AP. Helps offload congested APs and balance load in high-density environments.

802.11r — Fast BSS Transition (FT)

Pre-authenticates the client to the target AP before it roams. Reduces roaming handoff latency from ~200ms to under 50ms — critical for VoWi-Fi and real-time apps.

Controller-Based Roaming

A WLAN Controller (WLC) maintains client session state. When a client roams between APs, the controller transfers context invisibly — no re-authentication required.

Wireless Threat Vectors: Rogue APs, Evil Twins & DoS

▶

Rogue Access Point

An unauthorized AP plugged into the corporate wired network by an employee or attacker. Creates an unmanaged entry point into the internal network, bypassing perimeter security.

Evil Twin AP

An attacker broadcasts the same SSID as a legitimate corporate AP. Clients that connect are subjected to credential harvesting and man-in-the-middle decryption.

Deauthentication Flood (DoS)

802.11 management frames are unencrypted by default. An attacker spoofs deauth frames to force clients to disconnect repeatedly, causing a wireless denial of service.

RF Jamming

High-power noise on the Wi-Fi channel drowns out legitimate signals. All clients on the affected channel lose connectivity simultaneously.

WIPS & Protected Management Frames

▶

WIPS (Wireless Intrusion Prevention System) uses APs or dedicated sensors to continuously scan the RF environment for threats.

  • Rogue AP Detection: Compares scanned SSIDs and BSSIDs against an approved AP inventory. Flags unauthorized APs and can locate them via RF triangulation.
  • Evil Twin Detection: Identifies APs broadcasting a known corporate SSID with an unregistered BSSID or on an unexpected channel.
  • Containment: WIPS can transmit targeted deauthentication frames to disconnect clients from rogue APs — though this is itself an 802.11 management frame attack used defensively.

PMF (Protected Management Frames — 802.11w): Encrypts and authenticates management frames (deauth, disassoc) so attackers cannot spoof them. Required for WPA3 certification. Completely blocks deauthentication flood attacks when both AP and client support 802.11w.

802.1X Enterprise Flow:

Client (Supplicant) → AP (Authenticator) → RADIUS Server
1. Client associates to SSID
2. AP blocks all traffic except EAP
3. EAP exchange passes through AP to RADIUS (PEAP/EAP-TLS)
4. RADIUS issues Access-Accept + PMK
5. AP and client run 4-Way Handshake to derive session keys
6. AP opens full data port for the authenticated client

Multi-SSID Architecture, VLAN Segmentation & Guest Isolation

▶

Enterprise APs broadcast multiple SSIDs simultaneously, each mapped to a separate VLAN. This creates logical network separation on shared physical radio hardware.

SSIDVLANSecurityAccess
Corp-SecureVLAN 10WPA3 Enterprise (802.1X)Full internal network access via RBAC
Corp-BYODVLAN 20WPA2/3 Enterprise (802.1X)Internet + limited internal resources

Client Isolation: On guest SSIDs, enable AP-level client isolation so wireless devices on the same SSID cannot communicate directly with each other. All traffic is forced through the firewall for inspection.

Four Pillars of Enterprise WLANs

▶
01 / RF Foundation

Signal & Spectrum Design

Plan non-overlapping channels, target RSSI thresholds, and use beamforming to maximize signal quality. A failed RF design cannot be fixed in software.

02 / High-Density Performance

OFDMA, MU-MIMO & Wi-Fi 6

Use OFDMA and uplink MU-MIMO to serve hundreds of concurrent clients. Wi-Fi 6E and 7 move high-bandwidth clients to clean 6 GHz spectrum.

03 / Seamless Mobility

802.11k/v/r & Controller Roaming

Enable fast BSS transition protocols so VoWi-Fi and real-time apps survive AP handoffs without perceptible interruption.

04 / Zero Trust Wireless Security

WPA3 + 802.1X + WIPS + PMF

Authenticate every device individually with 802.1X, protect management frames with PMF, detect rogues with WIPS, and segment traffic via VLAN-mapped SSIDs.
