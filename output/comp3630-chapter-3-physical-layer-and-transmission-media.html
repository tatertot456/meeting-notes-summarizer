Bounded Media — Copper vs. Fiber

▶

Bounded media confines signals to a physical pathway — unlike wireless (unbounded), which radiates through open air. Two fundamental categories:

Copper Media (Electrical)

Propagates data as electrical voltage shifts through copper conductors. Types: twisted-pair (Ethernet LAN) and coaxial (broadband/RF). Vulnerable to EMI, RFI, crosstalk, and corrosion.

Optical Media (Light)

Transmits data as light pulses through glass or plastic cores. Types: multimode fiber (MMF, short range) and single-mode fiber (SMF, long range). 100% immune to EMI/RFI, zero crosstalk, electrically isolated, highly secure.

The fundamental tradeoff: copper is cheap and easy to terminate but degrades over distance and picks up interference. Fiber is expensive and requires specialized tools but spans continents and ignores all electromagnetic noise.

Twisted Pair Cabling

▶

Four pairs of insulated copper wires twisted together. Twisting neutralizes external EMI and internal crosstalk between pairs — the tighter the twist, the better the noise rejection.

Connectors:

  • 8P8C (RJ-45) — 8-position, 8-contact modular plug. Standard for Ethernet LAN.
  • RJ-11 — 6-position, 4-contact (6P4C). Standard for analog telephony.

Cable wiring configurations:

TypePin MappingUse Case
Straight-ThroughSame standard both ends (T568A↔T568A or T568B↔T568B)PC/printer to switch — connecting UNLIKE devices
CrossoverPins 1/2 swapped with 3/6 — TX and RX crossedSwitch-to-switch, PC-to-PC — connecting LIKE devices
Rollover / ConsolePin 1 connects to pin 8, fully reversedHost terminal to router/switch management console port

Modern switches with Auto-MDI/MDIX detect the cable type automatically and adjust internally — so crossover cables are rarely needed today. But you still need to know the distinction for exams and legacy equipment.

UTP vs. STP — Shielding

▶

UTP — Unshielded Twisted Pair

Outer PVC/LSZH jacket only — no metal shielding. Highly flexible, narrow, cheap, fast to terminate. Default choice for office drops, desktop workstations, IP phones, and indoor cameras. The dominant enterprise LAN cable.

STP — Shielded Twisted Pair

Adds metallic foil and/or braided copper shields around pairs or the whole bundle. ISO/IEC naming: F/UTP (foil around bundle), S/FTP (shielded bundle + foiled pairs). Required near generators, elevator motors, factory floors, and industrial machinery. Must be properly grounded — ungrounded shields act as antennas and make interference WORSE.

The 100-Meter Horizontal Channel Rule (TIA/EIA):

90m  Permanent horizontal link (solid core, inside walls/conduits)
+10m  Patch cords (stranded, PC-to-outlet + patch panel-to-switch)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
100m  Total channel limit — exceeding this causes signal degradation

Physical Impairments — Attenuation, Crosstalk & EMI

▶
ImpairmentDefinitionCauseMitigation
AttenuationGradual weakening of signal amplitude over distanceNatural resistance in copper. Lower AWG (thicker wire) = less resistance.Stay within 100m channel limit. Use repeaters/switches for longer runs.
NEXT (Near-End Crosstalk)Signal bleeds from one pair to adjacent pair, measured near transmitterElectromagnetic leakage between pairsMaintain tight twist rates. Don't untwist pairs more than allowed during termination.
FEXT (Far-End Crosstalk)Same as NEXT but measured at the receiver endSame electromagnetic leakageSame — proper termination technique
EMI / RFIExternal noise injected by electromagnetic or radio-frequency sourcesFluorescent lights, motors, transformers, power cables, radio transmittersUse STP in noisy environments. Proper cable routing away from power lines.

Proper installation technique matters almost as much as the cable category chosen. A Cat6A cable terminated sloppily — pairs untwisted too far, too close to power conduit — will perform worse than a well-installed Cat6 run. Certification testing after installation catches these problems before users do.

Coaxial Cable

▶

Four concentric layers from inside out:

1. Center Conductor  — inner copper wire carrying RF signals
2. Dielectric        — solid plastic/teflon spacing layer
3. Outer Shield      — braided metal mesh/foil blocking EMI
4. Outer Jacket      — physical and weather protection

Common types and uses:

TypeUse
RG-6Residential broadband internet, satellite drops, digital TV — modern standard
RG-11Long-distance carrier links and utility service entries — thick/heavy
RG-59Legacy analog CCTV security surveillance loops

DOCSIS (Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification) allows cable ISPs to deliver gigabit-class speeds over existing coax infrastructure using frequency-division multiplexing — multiple distinct RF channels coexist on a single cable simultaneously. This is why your cable internet uses the same physical wire as cable TV.

Fiber Optic Cable — MMF vs. SMF

▶

Five structural layers from inside out:

1. Glass Core     — silica glass tube carrying light
2. Cladding       — glass shield reflecting light back (total internal reflection)
3. Buffer         — plastic cushioning protecting the glass
4. Strength       — aramid (Kevlar) fibers preventing stretching
5. Jacket         — heavy-duty PVC outer protection

Total internal reflection — the physics that makes fiber work: light injected into the core cannot escape because the refractive index mismatch between core and cladding acts as an absolute mirror, bouncing light pulses forward.

Multimode Fiber (MMF)

Core diameter: 50–62.5 µm. Multiple light paths (modes) travel simultaneously. Modal dispersion limits distance — paths arrive at different times, pulses spread and overlap. Categories: OM1/OM2 (legacy), OM3/OM4/OM5 (laser-optimized, up to 100 Gbps). Use: under 500m, data centers, campus backbones.

Single-Mode Fiber (SMF)

Core diameter: ~9 µm. Core so narrow only one light path exists — no modal dispersion. Supports near-limitless distances and extreme bandwidth. Categories: OS1 (indoor), OS2 (long-haul carrier). Use: MAN/WAN, transoceanic cables, ISP trunks, multi-building campuses.

Modal dispersion is the core performance limiter of MMF. As the core gets larger, more light paths exist with more arrival time variance. SMF eliminates this entirely by making the core so small only one path is physically possible. This is why SMF spans continents while MMF tops out at ~500m.

Contact polishes — UPC vs APC:

UPC — Ultra Physical Contact (Blue)

Curved dome polish for direct physical contact. Standard for most LAN environments. Some reflected light returns down the core — acceptable for short distances.

APC — Angled Physical Contact (Green)

8-degree angled polish. Reflected light bounces into the cladding, not back down the core — maximizes return loss. Required for long-haul WAN, ISP, and carrier links where reflections cause serious signal degradation.

Never mate blue UPC with green APC connectors. The 8-degree angle mismatch physically damages both glass endfaces and causes catastrophic signal loss. Color coding exists precisely to prevent this mistake.

Bandwidth, Throughput & Goodput

▶
ConceptDefinitionExample
Nominal Capacity (Bandwidth)Theoretical maximum signaling rate under ideal conditions10G Ethernet link = 10,000 Mbps
ThroughputActual total data successfully crossing the medium — affected by congestion, errors, retransmissionsiperf3 reports 9.4 Gbps on a 10G link
GoodputUseful application-level data actually delivered after subtracting all protocol overheadWhat the user experiences downloading a file

Testing tools:

  • Copper certifier — measures wire map, length, attenuation, NEXT, return loss, delay. Confirms Cat6A compliance for 10GBASE-T.
  • iperf / iperf3 — active software tool measuring real TCP throughput between two endpoints.
  • Optical power meter — measures received optical power at fiber link endpoint.
  • Optical loss test set — measures total insertion loss over a fiber run.
  • OTDR (Optical Time Domain Reflectometer) — locates fiber breaks, splices, connector faults, tight bends, and maps distances graphically.

Structured Cabling — MDF, IDF & Distribution

▶

MDF — Main Distribution Frame

Central wiring rack near the carrier demarc. Houses core switches, primary routers, servers, and ISP equipment. Origin point for all inter-building backbone cabling.

IDF — Intermediate Distribution Frame

Distributed floor-level telecom closets. Links local endpoints back to MDF via high-speed backbone. Houses edge access switches, patch panels, and PoE switches.

Wall Drops & Patch Panels

Wall outlets connect endpoint clients to IDF patch panels. Patch panels link horizontal drop cabling to access switches. Punchdown blocks terminate copper; fiber panels terminate optical backbones.

Hierarchy Flow

Endpoint device → wall outlet → horizontal cable → IDF patch panel → access switch → backbone fiber → MDF core switch → router → ISP demarc.

Cable management in racks matters operationally. A well-organized rack (labeled cables, proper bend radius, velcro ties, consistent routing) can be traced and repaired in minutes. A rat's nest rack costs hours of downtime per incident and makes mistakes during maintenance far more likely.
