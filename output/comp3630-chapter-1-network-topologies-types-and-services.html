Two Pillars: Topology vs. Media

▶

Every network design rests on two foundational concepts that are distinct but work together:

Network Topology

How devices are arranged and communicate. Physical topology = the structural blueprint (cable locations, switch positions, endpoints). Logical topology = actual data traffic flow and access control rules. Think of it as: physical = empty streets, logical = traffic regulations.

Network Media

What physically carries the signal. Bounded (wired): copper (electrical signals) or fiber (light pulses). Unbounded (wireless): electromagnetic waves (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Cellular, Satellite). Think of it as: the physical highway asphalt that vehicles travel on.

Topology and media are independent choices. A star physical topology can run on twisted pair copper OR fiber. A wireless network has a star logical topology but uses unbounded media. Always evaluate both separately when designing a network.

Modern LANs almost universally use star topology because it isolates failures (one cable break affects only one device) and scales easily. Mesh is reserved for high-availability environments where redundancy justifies cost — the internet backbone is essentially a massive mesh.

Network Planes — Control, Data & Management

▶

Packet-switched networks (PSN) break data into packets transmitted via any available link — unlike legacy circuit-switched networks that reserve a dedicated physical path. To manage dynamic packet delivery, network nodes split operations into three planes:

Control Plane — The Brain

Calculates and decides HOW packets are routed. Builds and maintains routing tables. Runs protocols: OSPF, BGP, STP. Analogy: the city planner mapping optimal routes.

Data Plane — The Muscle

Actually FORWARDS packets based on control plane decisions. Handled in hardware (ASICs) by inspecting MAC addresses and routing headers. High-speed, low-level operation. Analogy: the vehicles driving freight along planned routes.

Management Plane — Administration

Monitors, configures, and manages network devices. Accessed by admins via SSH, SNMP, HTTPS portals, REST APIs, or local console. Analogy: the transit authority regulating and monitoring conditions.

The separation of planes is fundamental to modern networking — and to Software-Defined Networking (SDN), which physically separates the control plane from the data plane so a central controller manages routing decisions for many devices simultaneously. Understanding these three planes explains why network devices have separate management interfaces (not the same port you use for data traffic).

Hubs vs. Switches

▶

Ethernet Hub (Legacy)

Physical star, but LOGICAL BUS. Broadcasts every frame out of every port. All devices must inspect and discard frames not addressed to them. Heavy collision potential, serious security risk (easy to sniff all traffic), bandwidth shared across all devices.

Ethernet Switch (Modern)

Physical star AND logical point-to-point. Learns MAC addresses of connected devices dynamically. Forwards frames ONLY to the port where the destination MAC resides. Drastically fewer collisions, higher bandwidth, much better security.

Switches replaced hubs entirely in modern networks. The switch's MAC address table (CAM table) is the key — it maps each MAC address to a specific port. When a frame arrives, the switch looks up the destination MAC, finds the port, and sends it only there. Unknown MACs get flooded (like a hub) until learned. This single change from hub to switch transformed Ethernet from a collision-prone shared medium to a high-speed, secure, point-to-point network.

Network Models — P2P vs. Client-Server

▶

Peer-to-Peer (P2P)

Every host can be both client and server. No specialized central server OS required. Low hardware cost, simple setup. Poor scalability, difficult security enforcement. Best for: homes, small offices, fewer than 10 devices.

Client-Server

Dedicated central servers handle all requests. Centralized backups, authentication, and security policies. Highly scalable for large organizations. High cost and complex administration. Best for: modern enterprises, any organization needing centralized control.

WAN links are characterized by higher latency, lower throughput relative to cost, and shared infrastructure leased from carriers. LAN links are extremely fast (1–100 Gbps), low latency, and privately owned. The transition from LAN to WAN is where cost, complexity, and latency jump dramatically — this is where VPNs and SD-WAN become critical.

Overlay Technologies — VPN, SD-WAN, VLAN

▶

Overlay networks are logical networks built on top of physical infrastructure, adding encryption, routing intelligence, and segmentation that the physical underlay doesn't provide natively.

Physical WAN Underlay

Infrastructure spanning large distances — leased from telco carriers. Can use MPLS (label-based switching), dark fiber, microwave, or satellite. No native encryption or logical isolation.

VPN (Virtual Private Network)

Builds encrypted tunnels over public internet routes. Makes remote sites appear connected to the same private network. Low cost compared to dedicated leased lines.

SD-WAN

Software-Defined WAN. Secure overlays that automatically load-balance and route traffic across multiple connections (broadband, MPLS, LTE). Intelligent path selection based on application needs.

VLAN / VXLAN

VLAN: Logically segments a physical switch into multiple isolated networks. VXLAN: Extends layer 2 paths across cloud hypervisors and data center fabrics — scales to 16 million segments vs. VLAN's 4,096.

Service Providers & The Demarcation Point

▶

The demarcation point (demarc) is the precise boundary separating carrier-owned infrastructure from customer-owned equipment. It defines liability: the carrier owns everything up to the demarc, the customer owns everything after it.

Service Provider Side

High-capacity fiber/copper. Carrier Central Office (CO). Provider Edge (PE) routers. Carrier is responsible for all maintenance and uptime up to the demarc.

Demarc / Smartjack (NID)

The Network Interface Device at the customer premises. Acts as a smart interface and test point. Carrier can remotely perform loopback tests WITHOUT sending a technician on-site — isolates whether problems are on the carrier side or customer side.

Customer Premises (CPE)

Customer owns and is 100% responsible for: modems, ONTs (fiber termination), firewalls, routers, internal switches, and all internal cabling. Customer Premises Equipment = CPE.

Understanding the demarc is critical for troubleshooting. When a connection fails: test at the demarc first. If the carrier's loopback test passes, the problem is on the customer side (CPE). If it fails, the carrier must fix it. This clean division of responsibility prevents billing disputes and speeds up fault isolation.

Three architectural decisions that shape any network design:

  • Topology — star for LAN reliability, mesh/hybrid for high-availability backbones
  • Scale & Model — client-server for centralized control; VPN/SD-WAN to connect distributed sites
  • Media — twisted pair for workstation access, coaxial for broadband, fiber for backbones, wireless for mobility
